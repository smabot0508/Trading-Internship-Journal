## Core Idea Behind the Article

The author frames the whole exercise around a thought experiment shared by a member of the Compounding Quality investing community:

- Imagine you're given a large lump sum of money.
- You must invest all of it into **10 individual companies** (no ETFs allowed).
- That portfolio then goes into a trust you **cannot touch, sell, or change for 20 years**.
- Dividends are automatically reinvested.
- If a company gets acquired, you automatically receive an equivalent value of shares in the acquiring company.

**Why this exercise matters:** Because you can't trade in or out of positions, the goal shifts completely. You're no longer trying to maximize returns by being clever — you're trying to **avoid picking a company that could permanently fail or become irrelevant**. This lines up with Warren Buffett's well-known investing principle: protecting capital from permanent loss matters more than chasing the biggest possible gain.

This is a useful mental model for evaluating any long-term holding: ask not "can this stock go up a lot?" but "is there any realistic way this business becomes worthless or obsolete in 20 years?"

## The 10 Companies (as far as the free preview goes)

### 10. W.W. Grainger ($GWW)
- **Business model:** Sells maintenance, repair, and operating (MRO) supplies — everything from safety goggles to industrial motors — to millions of businesses and institutions worldwide.
- **Why it should last 20 years:**
  - Massive scale and distribution network keep their costs lower than smaller competitors.
  - Carries the widest product selection, making it a "one-stop shop" for industrial buyers.
  - Deeply embedded in B2B supply chains and facility maintenance workflows.
  - As long as physical businesses exist, they'll need tools, spare parts, and safety equipment.

### 9. Sherwin-Williams ($SHW)
- **Business model:** Manufactures and distributes paint, coatings, and related supplies, mostly through its own network of company-owned stores that serve professional contractors directly.
- **Why it should last 20 years:**
  - Paint and protective coatings are a permanent need for maintaining infrastructure and housing.
  - The distribution network (thousands of stores) is very difficult for a new competitor to replicate.
  - Because time is money for contractors, having a store nearby builds strong loyalty to the brand.

### 8. Cintas ($CTS)
- **Business model:** Rents and cleans corporate uniforms and floor mats, and restocks restroom/first-aid supplies for businesses.
- **Why it should last 20 years:**
  - Operates at huge local scale — more than 12,000 delivery routes.
  - Once a business becomes a customer, switching providers is inconvenient, so retention is very high.
  - As long as workplaces exist, there will be recurring demand for uniforms, safety gear, and facility supplies.

### 7. Rollins ($ROL)
- **Business model:** Parent company of Orkin; provides residential and commercial pest control through a recurring subscription model.
- **Why it should last 20 years:**
  - Pests (termites, rodents, insects) are a permanent, recurring problem that requires repeat treatment.
  - Historically resilient in recessions — pest control is one of the last services people cancel.
  - The pest-control industry is still fragmented among many small operators, giving Rollins a long runway to keep acquiring competitors and growing.

### 6. S&P Global ($SPGI)
- **Business model:** Provides credit ratings, financial benchmarks (like the S&P 500 index), and data analytics to global capital markets.
- **Why it should last 20 years:**
  - One of only a handful of dominant players in the global ratings/benchmarks market.
  - Companies that borrow money need credit ratings — the financial system depends on them.
  - As long as capital markets exist, S&P Global earns a cut of financial data and transaction activity.

### 5. Mastercard ($MA)
- **Business model:** Operates one of the world's largest digital payment networks, earning a small fee/percentage on every card transaction.
- **Why it should last 20 years:**
  - Classic **network effect**: merchants accept Mastercard because shoppers use it, and shoppers use it because merchants accept it.
  - Takes on **no credit risk** — it simply operates the "toll road" that payments travel through, rather than lending money itself.
  - The continued global shift away from cash keeps pushing more transaction volume onto Mastercard's network.

### 4. Brookfield Corporation ($BN)
- **Business model:** An alternative asset manager that owns and operates large, cash-generating real assets globally — toll roads, hydroelectric dams, and premium real estate.
- **Why it should last 20 years:**
  - Owns physical infrastructure that the global economy depends on and that can't easily be replaced.
  - Much of its revenue comes from long-term contracts (20–50 years) that are often inflation-linked.
  - Management has a strong track record of buying undervalued/troubled assets, improving them, and redeploying the proceeds.

## Common Threads Across These Picks

Looking at the 7 companies available, a clear pattern in the author's selection criteria emerges:

1. **Distribution/scale moats** — Grainger, Sherwin-Williams, and Cintas all win primarily through massive networks that are expensive and slow for a competitor to copy.
2. **Recurring, non-discretionary demand** — Rollins (pest control) and Cintas (uniforms/supplies) both benefit from services businesses can't easily skip, even in a downturn.
3. **Network effects / toll-road economics** — Mastercard and S&P Global both sit "in the middle" of transactions or capital markets, collecting a small fee on enormous volume without taking on much risk themselves.
4. **Hard-to-replace real assets** — Brookfield owns infrastructure (dams, toll roads, real estate) that simply can't be recreated easily, with long-duration, inflation-linked contracts.

## My Takeaways / Questions for Further Study

- All 7 visible picks are **"boring" businesses** — none are high-growth tech names. The thesis leans heavily on durability and moats rather than explosive growth.
- Worth researching: how much of each company's revenue is **recurring vs. one-time**, since that's the backbone of the "hold for 20 years" argument.
