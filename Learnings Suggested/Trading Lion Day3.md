## Overview

This session was less about a single chart pattern and more about **how three very different trading styles all converge on the same core disciplines**: extremely precise entries, unforgiving risk control, and constant awareness of where the broader market cycle stands. Getkaker shorts parabolic blow-off tops, Prisbee runs concentrated long momentum positions with surgical position sizing, and Clement A specializes in buying market leaders right at the tail end of corrections — three opposite-sounding styles that all still obey the same underlying rules around cutting losses fast and sizing risk deliberately.

## 1. Professional Trading Strategies

### 1.1 Phil Getkaker — Parabolic Short Selling
Getkaker specializes in **shorting parabolic stocks and writing options**, and considers technical analysis to be **90–95% of the equation** for short-term trading — in his view, price can deviate so far from a company's actual fundamental value in the short term that fundamentals become almost irrelevant to the trade itself.

- **A+ Setups:** He looks for stocks on **multi-day or multi-week momentum runs that have become extremely overextended** — what he calls an "overinflated balloon." Historical examples cited include the massive runs in **Super Micro Computer** and **MicroStrategy**.
- **Precision Entries and Scaling:** Rather than guessing the exact top, he waits for **momentum to visibly break and exhaustion to show, ideally on record volume**. Once the stock starts falling and the trade is already working, he **scales into the position** by adding more size — building into strength on the short side rather than trying to front-run the top.
- **"Paper Cut Losses":** If a stock breaks back above his designated resistance level, he **cuts the loss immediately** rather than holding stubbornly, on the logic that he can always re-enter later if the setup reappears. This is the central risk-management idea of his whole approach (expanded on in Section 2).

### 1.2 Shake Prisbee — Dynamic Position Sizing and Aggressive Momentum
Prisbee runs a **highly concentrated, risk-on momentum strategy** built for trending markets — the goal is to build very large portfolio weightings in leading stocks while keeping actual **account-level risk** extremely tight.

- **Maximizing Size Through Micro Entries:** He identifies **macro setups** (like weekly chart breakouts) but executes using **precise intraday inflection points** — 30-minute pivots or VWAP reclaims — which lets him compress his stop-loss down to roughly **2% instead of a classic 8%**. This is what allows him to put **20–40% of his portfolio into a single trade** while risking only **0.5–1.5% of total account equity**. (Full mechanics in Section 4.)
- **Taking Profit into Strength:** To manage risk on an already-large position, he systematically **sells half his position into strength at 5–10R** (multiples of his original risk), locking in a large gain while keeping a smaller "core" position to ride the longer-term trend.
- **Tracking External Factors:** Prisbee actively watches **foreign markets and macro indicators** to get an early read on US price action — for example, monitoring the **South Korean KOSPI overnight** as a leading indicator for US memory-chip stocks like **Micron**, or watching the **US Dollar (DXY)** to anticipate moves in commodities and metals.
- **First Test of the 20 EMA:** One of his signature setups is **buying the very first test of the 20-day EMA** during a strong momentum run — he's observed this level frequently acts as the low for weeks at a time during a healthy trend.

### 1.3 Clement A — Post-Correction Position Trading
Clement A focuses on **positional trading**, specifically getting heavily involved right at the **tail end of market corrections** to capture the early, cleanest part of the next move.

- **Spotting Relative Strength:** He identifies future market leaders by looking for **relative strength during a market downturn** — stocks building constructive bases or forming **higher lows** while the broader index (e.g., the Nasdaq) is still making **lower lows**.
- **Situational Awareness and Aggression:** His core principle is to be **most aggressive at the start of a new cycle**, since breakouts follow through most easily right after a correction, when the market is still "uncrowded." As the rally matures and second-tier/laggard stocks start running too, he reads that as a sign the cycle is getting **crowded**, and scales back his risk accordingly.
- **Multi-Timeframe Execution:** He looks for **daily-chart confluence** — for example, a stock pulling back into its 20-day or 50-day moving average — then zooms into a **5-minute "6/20 MACD" chart** to time the precise intraday turn. (More detail in Section 3.)

### 1.4 Universal Principles Across All Three
Despite very different setups, all three traders converge on the same handful of rules:
- **Relentless trade analysis:** Getkaker reportedly **doubled his P&L** simply by studying his own tax returns, identifying which setups were actually his best performers, and cutting out the low-probability trades that had been quietly draining the account.
- **Capital and mental preservation:** All three agree the market only offers "easy," high-momentum conditions **two or three times a year**. In choppy or downtrending environments, the professional move is to **drastically reduce size, step back, and preserve both financial and mental capital** until conditions turn favorable again.

## 2. Risk Management

Across all three traders, risk management is described as something much bigger than "where do I place my stop" — it's an all-encompassing system covering psychology, position sizing, and awareness of the broader market cycle.

### 2.1 The Golden Rule: Paper Cuts and Psychological Preservation
Getkaker treats **the willingness to admit you're wrong and cut a loss immediately** as the single most important rule in trading. His framing: a trader can absorb an unlimited number of small "paper cuts," but **letting one loss grow too large is an amputation** — a single mistake big enough to seriously damage the account.

The danger of a large drawdown (20–30%) isn't just financial — it's **psychological**. Big losses tend to trigger **revenge trading**, which snowballs into further emotional mistakes. To keep this in check, Getkaker suggests **treating your available buying power as if it were the last dollar you have** before placing any trade — a mental framing device meant to force genuine caution rather than casual risk-taking.

Clement A frames a related idea as **"path dependency"**: poor risk management during a choppy, difficult market period **depletes the mental capital** a trader needs later, once the easy, favorable conditions return. In other words, how you handle the hard stretches directly determines how well you're able to capitalize on the easy ones.

### 2.2 Dynamic Sizing and Managing "Portfolio Heat"
Prisbee's approach to risk is built around **surgically compressing stop-losses** so he can run much larger position sizes without exposing the overall account to real danger:
- **Intraday precision for macro trends:** entering at precise intraday inflection points (30-minute pivots) shrinks a classic 8% swing-trade stop down to roughly **2%**, which is what unlocks the ability to allocate up to **40% of the portfolio** into a single breakout while keeping true account risk to just **0.5–1.5%**.
- **Mitigating overnight risk ("day one price distance from cost"):** because his positions are so large, he requires the stock to close with enough of a cushion above his entry on the very first day. If it doesn't, he **trims the position down to 15–20%** specifically to protect against an overnight gap-down — in his words, to make sure he "lives to see the next day."
- **Managing heat by trimming into strength:** holding an oversized position creates real psychological pressure ("portfolio heat") during a normal pullback. His fix is to **systematically sell half the position at 5–10R**, locking in a large gain and freeing himself mentally to hold the remaining "core" position through the rest of the trend.

### 2.3 Cycle Awareness and Progressive Exposure
Risk management also governs **when** to deploy capital at all, not just how much:
- Clement A's rule of being most aggressive **right after a correction** (Section 1.3) is itself a risk-management decision — trades are cleaner and less crowded early in a new cycle, so the same position size carries genuinely lower risk than it would once the cycle matures and laggards start participating.
- When the environment turns choppy or a trend breaks down, professionals **don't force trades**. Prisbee is willing to sit in **100% cash for weeks** if his trend filters are invalidated, and Getkaker insists on sizing down drastically to trade for "scraps" until performance and conditions realign.

### 2.4 Long-Term Financial Risk: Wiring Out Profits
Perhaps the highest-level risk management principle covered: **separating trading capital from overall net worth.** Getkaker advocates **wiring profits out of the trading account regularly — often monthly** — and redirecting them into safer, passive investments (real estate, fixed income, index funds). The logic: even in the event of a catastrophic drawdown in the trading account itself, the trader's actual accumulated wealth and livelihood remain protected, because it's no longer sitting inside the account that's exposed to trading risk.

## 3. Technical Chart Analysis

Technical analysis is described as the **primary driver of execution and risk management** for these traders — for short-term trading specifically, Getkaker puts the weighting at 90–95% technical versus fundamental.

### 3.1 Mixing the "Micro with the Macro" (Multi-Timeframe Analysis)
A theme shared by Prisbee and Clement A: build the **core thesis on a large timeframe** (weekly or daily charts), but **execute the actual entry on a much smaller, surgical timeframe**.
- **The macro setup:** identifying large structural patterns — weekly bases, multi-week consolidations — to establish the stock's overall "story" and potential.
- **The micro execution:** zooming in drastically to actually place the trade. Prisbee often uses 30-minute or 65-minute pivots, and sometimes even 2-minute charts, to pinpoint a precise "make or break" support level. Clement uses a specific **5-minute "6/20 MACD" chart** (a MACD built from 6-period and 20-period inputs) to watch for intraday price stabilization, moving-average crosses, and MACD crossovers before entering.
- **The payoff:** this combination lets a trader participate in a large weekly-chart breakout while still keeping the stop-loss very tight (the same 2%-vs-8% dynamic described in Section 2.2).

### 3.2 Optimizing Classic Technical Analysis
Because standard setups (like a textbook cup-and-handle breakout) are so widely known and heavily traded by retail algorithms, the "obvious" buy point often just produces **breakout-area chop and fakeouts** rather than a clean move.
- **Buying the first test, not the breakout:** Prisbee avoids the crowded breakout level entirely, instead buying **deeper intraday inflection points** — most notably the **"first test of the 20" (20-day moving average)** during a strong trend, a pattern he identified by studying historical analogs. He applies a similar idea to the **first test of the 50-day moving average** for gap-down reclaims.
- **Finding confluence:** Clement A looks specifically for **"layers of support"** — pullbacks where multiple technical levels line up at once, such as a prior breakout level now acting as support, coinciding with both the 20-day EMA and the 200-day SMA. The more independent reasons a level has to hold, the more confidence in the trade.

### 3.3 Reading Price Action, Volume, and Volatility
Several of these traders strip away most indicators entirely and read **pure price and volume**.
- **Price and volume purity:** Getkaker uses no traditional indicators — when shorting a parabolic chart, he waits specifically for a **"climax day"**: a massive opening shove higher that immediately reverses on record-breaking volume, signaling sellers have fully overwhelmed buyers.
- **Tight vs. wide-and-loose price action:** Clement A judges the health of a trend by the **"character"** of its price bars. A **tight** consolidation (e.g., a 17–19% flag with more up days than down days) suggests institutional control and a healthy setup. A **wide and loose** consolidation (e.g., a 35% deep range full of erratic gaps in both directions) signals a loss of institutional control — his cue to step away from that name.
- **Intraday volume surges:** Prisbee watches for volume spikes on very short timeframes (even 2-minute charts) as a signal that large institutional buyers are stepping in, using that surge as the trigger to build his position size.

### 3.4 Structural Relative Strength (RS)
During a market correction, technical analysis becomes the tool for finding the **next cycle's leaders**. Clement A's structural definition: if the Nasdaq or S&P 500 is making a **lower low**, but an individual stock is instead forming a **higher low**, that stock is technically diverging and showing genuine relative strength. Similarly, a stock holding **above its own 50-day moving average** while the index trades **below its own 50-day** is a sign of real institutional accumulation happening underneath a weak tape.

### 3.5 Unrelenting Historical Pattern Study
The foundation underneath all of this: **relentless historical chart review.** Getkaker has reportedly studied **over a million charts**, and keeps a physical binder of hundreds of printed "A+" setups from past years. The purpose of this repetition is to train the eye to recognize **exact intraday breakdown patterns and volume signatures** instantly, across any timeframe, so that a new setup mirroring a past winner (or loser) is recognized in real time rather than after the fact.

## 4. Dynamic Position Sizing (Deep Dive)

This section expands on Shake Prisbee's signature strategy — using precision entries to justify much larger-than-normal position sizes, while keeping true account-level risk small.

### 4.1 The Math: Micro Entries for Macro Sizing
A standard weekly-breakout trade with a classic **8% stop-loss** mathematically limits how large a position can be while keeping risk reasonable. Prisbee's fix is to enter the same macro setup using a **surgical intraday inflection point** (a 30-minute pivot or a VWAP reclaim), which shrinks the effective stop down to roughly **2%**. Because the stop is four times tighter, he can allocate a **20–40% portfolio weighting** to a single position while still keeping total account risk to just **0.5–1.5%** — the tighter, more precise entry is what makes the larger size mathematically justifiable.

### 4.2 Mitigating Overnight Risk: "Day One Price Distance"
Holding 40% of a portfolio in one high-beta stock overnight is inherently risky because of gap-down potential. Prisbee's safeguard is requiring **"day one price distance from cost"** — the stock needs to close with a comfortable enough cushion above his entry on the very first day. If it doesn't provide that buffer, he **dynamically trims the position down to 15–20%** specifically to reduce his exposure to an overnight gap.

### 4.3 Taking Profit into Strength
When an oversized position moves quickly in his favor, it creates significant **"portfolio heat"** — a normal market pullback will now swing the open profit violently, which tends to provoke panicked, emotional selling. The fix is systematic: **sell 50% of the position into strength**, typically once it reaches **5–10R** (multiples of the original risk). Banking that gain reduces the position back down to a comfortable **10–20%** weighting, which lets him mentally detach from the trade and simply trail the remaining "core" position along the moving averages — largely immune to the fear of a routine 10–15% pullback.

### 4.4 Managing Total Portfolio Heat
Dynamic sizing isn't just about individual trades — it requires constantly evaluating the **aggregate risk across the whole portfolio**. Prisbee has described learning this lesson the hard way during a position in **AAOI**, where he held multiple oversized, *correlated* positions right as the market became extended. When high-beta stocks then dropped 10–15% quickly, the combined "heat" across all those correlated positions became mentally overwhelming, and he ended up emotionally selling a winning position right at its exact low. Since then, he **calculates the exact worst-case drawdown his entire portfolio could suffer every single night**, to make sure his sizing genuinely matches his real risk tolerance — not just what feels comfortable in a calm market.

### 4.5 Drastic Adjustments Based on External Factors
Dynamic sizing also means being willing to **rapidly de-risk** the whole portfolio based on external signals, not just price action in the stocks themselves. Prisbee's example: after a euphoric run where the S&P 500 had risen for **10 straight weeks**, he spotted a sharp bearish reversal overnight in the **South Korean KOSPI** — which he treats as a leading indicator for US semiconductor stocks. Recognizing the signal, he moved from being **150% invested on margin down to 80%, and eventually down to just 30%** over the following days. That fast de-risking allowed him to **keep 93% of his peak equity gains** through the subsequent market selloff.

### 4.6 The "One Share" Tactic
A smaller but clever piece of the sizing toolkit: **buying exactly one share** of a stock he's watching. In a hot market with thousands of potential setups, it's easy to lose track of a top-tier leader that hasn't set up cleanly yet. Owning a single share keeps that name **visible in his active portfolio P&L every day**, so it doesn't get forgotten. When the stock eventually presents a genuinely low-risk entry, he then scales that single share dynamically up into a full **30–40% portfolio weighting**.

## Key Takeaways

1. Three very different trading styles (parabolic shorting, concentrated long momentum, post-correction positioning) all converge on the same non-negotiables: cut losses fast, size risk deliberately, and stay aware of where the broader cycle stands.
2. Risk management is bigger than a stop-loss placement — it spans psychology (paper cuts, "last dollar" framing), position sizing (dynamic sizing, portfolio heat), and long-term financial protection (wiring out profits monthly).
3. Dynamic position sizing's core insight is that **a tighter, more precise entry mathematically justifies a larger position size** — but only when paired with explicit safeguards against overnight risk (day-one price distance) and portfolio-wide correlation risk.
4. Technical analysis across all three traders leans heavily on **multi-timeframe execution** (macro thesis, micro entry) and **reading the character of price action** (tight vs. wide-and-loose) over relying on standard indicators.
